    3Power Project: predicting lagging power factor

    Independent project, April 2024. Python, pandas, scikit-learn, Power BI. View the repository

    • Lagging power factor measures how far a system drifts from delivering power efficiently, and it is subject to federal power factor requirements.
    • Models lagging power factor from a year of meter-style readings at a steel plant and identifies what drives it.
    • Carries the work from exploratory analysis through model selection, tuning, and a Power BI report.

    3.1 Data

    • The Steel Industry Data set holds 35,040 readings taken at 15-minute intervals throughout 2018.
    • Each reading records energy usage, lagging and leading reactive power, CO₂, both power factors, seconds since midnight, and the day and load type.
    • No null handling was needed, and outliers were kept because they carry the inefficiency events the model needs to learn.
    • Derived active power in watts for each interval, label encoded the categorical fields, and split the date into day, month, and year.
    • Split the data 64/16/20 into training, validation, and test sets.

    3.2 Methods

    • Compared three regression models chosen for their tolerance of outliers, multicollinearity, and unscaled data: ridge regression, random forest, and gradient boosting.
    • Tuned each with a grid search using three-fold cross-validation and scored it by RMSE on the validation set.
    • Selected random forest and tuned it again with five-fold cross-validation around its best parameters: maximum depth 19, 150 estimators, and a minimum split of 2.
    Table 1. Model comparison. RMSE is in power factor percentage points; the miss count is predictions above 90% where the actual value fell below 90%.
    ModelEvaluation setRMSEMissed low-PF readings
    Ridge regressionValidation9.1397
    Gradient boostingValidation0.6014
    Random forestValidation0.546
    Random forest, finalTest0.556

    3.3 Results

    • The final random forest predicted lagging power factor on unseen test data with an RMSE of 0.55 percentage points.
    • It was chosen over gradient boosting because it scored slightly better, is lighter to run and maintain, and exposes feature importances that make the result explainable.
    • The strongest predictors were time of day, lagging reactive power, active power, usage, leading reactive power, and month.
    • Power factor had a non-linear relationship with time of day, dropping lowest in the early morning and at night.

    3.4 Limitations and next steps

    • Lagging reactive power is part of how power factor is calculated, so including it as a feature inflates performance.
    • A next iteration would retrain without it, add polynomial features before revisiting ridge regression, and move to time series models for usage and power factor.
    • Unit-level metering would help locate where in the system the inefficiencies occur.

      2IEEE Std 738 in brief

      IEEE Std 738 [1] calculates the relationship between current and temperature in bare overhead conductors. At steady state, the heat a conductor gains from its own resistance and from the sun equals the heat it loses to the surrounding air:

      I2 R(Tc) + qs = qc + qr (1)

      Here I is current, R(Tc) is the conductor's resistance at temperature Tc, qs is solar heat gain, qc is convective cooling from wind or rising air, and qr is radiative cooling, all per unit length. Solving for I at the conductor's maximum allowable temperature gives its thermal rating; solving for Tc at a known current gives its operating temperature.

      That one line hides a lot. The standard rolls more than 35 separate calculations into the balance, covering air properties, three convection regimes, solar geometry and heat flux, conductor resistance, heat capacity, and transient response.

      Ethan's pipeline applies this network-wide across five years of data, more than 9 billion records. For each recorded amperage it calculates conductor temperature by minimizing the heat-balance residual, the same kind of search shown with COBYLA on the Fun and Games tab.

      S1Solving for conductor temperature with COBYLA

      Given a current, how hot does the line run? There is no closed-form answer, so an optimizer searches for the temperature where heat in equals heat out. COBYLA (Constrained Optimization BY Linear Approximation) [1] does this using only function values, fitting a straight-line model inside a trust region and shrinking that region as it closes in. Here it adjusts the conductor temperature until the current that temperature implies matches the line's recorded current. Set the conditions, then press Run to watch it converge.

      Steps to converge

      1. Make the initial guess

        Start at the midpoint of the normalized search space.

        In production: the initial guess would be a temperature approximation, estimated from the line's real loading, rather than the midpoint.

        x0 = 0.5,   T(x) = Ta + x(Tmax − Ta)

      2. Establish the trust region

        Set the starting radius and evaluate a second point one radius away to form the first simplex.

        ρ = ρbeg = 0.09,   x1 = x0 + ρ

      3. Evaluate the amperage residual

        At a candidate temperature, compute the current the heat balance supports and compare it with the recorded current.

        r(x) = √[(qc + qr − qs) / R(T)] − Irec

      4. Build the linear model

        Fit a straight line through the best point and the most recent other point.

        r̂(xb + d) = r(xb) + g d,   g = [r(xo) − r(xb)] / (xo − xb)

      5. Solve the trust-region step

        Step to where the model reaches zero, limited to the trust region and to the linearized bounds of the search space.

        d = clip(−r(xb) / g, −ρ, ρ),   0 ≤ xb + d ≤ 1

      6. Compare with the penalized merit

        Accept the trial point only if it improves the merit, which penalizes leaving the search space.

        φ(x) = |r(x)| + σ Σ max(0, −ci(x)),   accept if φ(xb + d) < φ(xb)

      7. Update the trust region

        Shrink the radius when the model predicted poorly or the step was short. It never grows.

        ratio = Δφactual / Δφpredicted;   if ratio < 0.1 or |d| < ρ/2:   ρ ← ρ/2

      8. Check convergence

        Stop when an accepted step barely improves the merit, or the trust region becomes tiny.

        Δφ < ftol = 10−7   or   ρ < xtol = 10−6

      9. Report the result

        Convert back to temperature and report the residual, iteration count, and solve time.

        T* = T(xb),   RMSE = |r(xb)| (A)

      S3How much current can a power line carry?

      A conductor's rating depends on the weather. Wind cools it, sun heats it, and hot air limits how much heat it can shed. Move the sliders to see how the steady-state rating of a common conductor changes. This is a simplified version of the IEEE 738 calculation [2] used in the thermal ratings pipeline.

      What is an ambient-adjusted rating? An ambient-adjusted rating (AAR) is a line rating recalculated from the forecast air temperature, typically every hour, instead of one fixed, conservative seasonal temperature. Wind and sunlight stay at conservative assumed values, so an AAR changes with the temperature alone. FERC Order No. 881 requires most U.S. transmission providers to use AARs.

      How does IEEE 738 produce dynamic line ratings? A dynamic line rating (DLR) runs the same heat balance continuously with measured or forecast conditions along the line: wind speed and direction, air temperature, and solar radiation, often checked against sensors that measure conductor temperature, tension, or sag. For each time step and line span, the calculation solves for the current that keeps the conductor at its maximum allowable temperature, and the lowest result along the line becomes that interval's rating. The calculator below is one what-if step of that process, not a DLR on its own.

      Calculations in IEEE Std 738

      37 calculations roll up into the standard's heat balance, shown in SI units: temperatures in °C, lengths in m, heat in W/m. Symbols follow the standard: Ts surface, Ta air, D0 conductor diameter, Vw wind speed, He elevation, N day of year. See IEEE Std 738-2023 for the authoritative formulations and coefficient tables before any engineering use.

      Heat balance and ratings

      1. Steady-state heat balanceqc + qr = qs + I2R(Tavg)
      2. Steady-state thermal rating (current at the maximum conductor temperature)I = √[(qc + qr − qs) / R(Tavg)]
      3. Conductor temperature for a known current (iterative solution)solve qc(T) + qr(T) − qs − I2R(T) = 0 for T
      4. Transient heat balance for time-varying current and weatherdTavg/dt = [R(Tavg)I2 + qs − qc − qr] / (mCp)
      5. Conductor temperature stepped through timeTavg(t + Δt) = Tavg(t) + (dTavg/dt) Δt
      6. Transient thermal ratingfind If such that Tavg(tf) = Tmax after a step from Ii

      Convective cooling

      1. Forced convection at low wind speedqc1 = Kangle [1.01 + 1.35 NRe0.52] kf (Ts − Ta)
      2. Forced convection at high wind speedqc2 = Kangle · 0.754 NRe0.6 kf (Ts − Ta)
      3. Natural convection with no windqcn = 3.645 ρf0.5 D00.75 (Ts − Ta)1.25
      4. Selection of the governing convection termqc = max[qcn, qc(Vw)],   qc(Vw) = max(qc1, qc2)
      5. Reynolds numberNRe = D0 ρf Vw / μf
      6. Wind direction factorKangle = 1.194 − cos(φ) + 0.194 cos(2φ) + 0.368 sin(2φ)
      7. Air film temperatureTfilm = (Ts + Ta) / 2
      8. Dynamic viscosity of airμf = 1.458 × 10−6 (Tfilm + 273)1.5 / (Tfilm + 383.4)
      9. Air density with elevationρf = (1.293 − 1.525 × 10−4 He + 6.379 × 10−9 He2) / (1 + 0.00367 Tfilm)
      10. Thermal conductivity of airkf = 2.424 × 10−2 + 7.477 × 10−5 Tfilm − 4.407 × 10−9 Tfilm2

      Radiative cooling

      1. Radiated heat lossqr = 17.8 D0 ε [((Ts + 273) / 100)4 − ((Ta + 273) / 100)4]

      Solar heating

      1. Solar heat gainqs = α Qse sin(θ) A′
      2. Effective angle of incidence of the sun's raysθ = arccos[cos(Hc) cos(Zc − Zl)]
      3. Solar altitudeHc = arcsin[cos(Lat) cos(δ) cos(ω) + sin(Lat) sin(δ)]
      4. Solar azimuthZc = C + arctan(χ)
      5. Solar azimuth variableχ = sin(ω) / [sin(Lat) cos(ω) − cos(Lat) tan(δ)]
      6. Solar azimuth constantC = 0°, 180°, or 360°, selected from the hour angle ω and the sign of χ
      7. Solar declinationδ = 23.46 sin[(284 + N) / 365 × 360°]
      8. Hour angleω = (solar hour − 12) × 15°
      9. Total solar and sky heat flux, clear atmosphereQs = A + BHc + CHc2 + DHc3 + EHc4 + FHc5 + GHc6 (clear coefficients)
      10. Total solar and sky heat flux, industrial atmosphereQs = A + BHc + … + GHc6 (industrial coefficients)
      11. Solar heat flux elevation correctionQse = Ksolar Qs,   Ksolar = 1 + 1.148 × 10−4 He − 1.108 × 10−8 He2
      12. Projected area of the conductorA′ = D0 (per unit length)

      Conductor properties

      1. AC resistance at conductor temperature (linear interpolation)R(T) = [(Rhigh − Rlow) / (Thigh − Tlow)] (T − Tlow) + Rlow
      2. Resistance extrapolation above the highest tabulated temperaturethe same linear form, extended beyond Thigh
      3. Heat capacity of the conductor as the sum of its materialsmCp = Σ mi Cpi
      4. Specific heat of each material at temperatureCp(T) = Cp(20 °C) [1 + β(T − 20)]

      Temperature distribution

      1. Radial temperature difference between core and surfaceTcore − Ts = [PT / (2π kth)] [½ − (Dcore2 / (D02 − Dcore2)) ln(D0 / Dcore)]
      2. Average conductor temperature from core and surfaceTavg = (Tcore + Ts) / 2
      3. Effective radial thermal conductivitykth, an empirical value for the stranded conductor used in the radial difference above
      4. Thermal time constantτ = mCp (Tf − Ti) / [R(Tf)If2 − R(Ti)Ii2]
