    1Power project: predicting lagging power factor

    Independent project, April 2024. Python, pandas, scikit-learn, Power BI. View the repository

    Lagging power factor measures how far a system drifts from delivering power efficiently, and it is subject to federal power factor requirements. This project uses a year of meter-style readings from a steel plant to model lagging power factor and identify what drives it, carrying the work from exploratory analysis through model selection, tuning, and a Power BI report.

    1.1 Data

    The Steel Industry Data set contains 35,040 readings taken at 15-minute intervals throughout 2018. Each reading records energy usage, lagging and leading reactive power, CO₂, both power factors, seconds since midnight, and the day and load type. The data needed no null handling, and outliers were kept because they carry the inefficiency events the model needs to learn. Active power in watts was derived from usage for each interval, categorical fields were label encoded, and the date was split into day, month, and year. The data was then split 64/16/20 into training, validation, and test sets.

    1.2 Methods

    Three regression models were compared, each chosen for its tolerance of outliers, multicollinearity, and unscaled data: ridge regression, random forest, and gradient boosting. Each was tuned with a grid search using three-fold cross-validation and scored by RMSE on the validation set. Random forest was selected and tuned again with five-fold cross-validation around its best parameters, settling on a maximum depth of 19, 150 estimators, and a minimum split of 2.

    Table 1. Model comparison. RMSE is in power factor percentage points; the miss count is predictions above 90% where the actual value fell below 90%.
    ModelEvaluation setRMSEMissed low-PF readings
    Ridge regressionValidation9.1397
    Gradient boostingValidation0.6014

    1.3 Results

    The final random forest predicted lagging power factor on unseen test data with an RMSE of 0.55 percentage points. It was chosen over gradient boosting because it scored slightly better, is lighter to run and maintain, and exposes feature importances that make the result explainable. The strongest predictors were time of day, lagging reactive power, active power, usage, leading reactive power, and month. Power factor had a non-linear relationship with time of day, dropping lowest in the early morning and at night.

    1.4 Limitations and next steps

    Lagging reactive power is part of how power factor is calculated, so including it as a feature inflates performance. A next iteration would retrain without it, add polynomial features before revisiting ridge regression, and move to time series models for usage and power factor. Unit-level metering would help locate where in the system the inefficiencies occur.

    2IEEE Std 738 in brief

    IEEE Std 738 calculates the relationship between current and temperature in bare overhead conductors. At steady state, the heat a conductor gains from its own resistance and from the sun equals the heat it loses to the surrounding air:

    I2 R(Tc) + qs = qc + qr (1)

    Here I is current, R(Tc) is the conductor's resistance at temperature Tc, qs is solar heat gain, qc is convective cooling from wind or rising air, and qr is radiative cooling, all per unit length. Solving for I at the conductor's maximum allowable temperature gives its thermal rating; solving for Tc at a known current gives its operating temperature.

    Ethan's pipeline applies this calculation network-wide across five years of data, more than 9 billion records. A simplified, interactive version is on the Fun and Games tab.
