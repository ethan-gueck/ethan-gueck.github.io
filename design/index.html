Design Philosophy

How the portfolio looks and reads, and why: one colour family on tinted paper, a single accent, two typefaces, shallow depth and a measured amount to take in.

Contents

    1The idea: a journal on paper

    The portfolio is laid out like a research journal: a masthead, numbered sections, figures with captions and a reference list. The palette serves that page rather than competing with it.

    • One brand family, one accent. Every brand colour is a single hue stepped in lightness; one warm accent marks what matters (section numbers, active states, the call to action).
    • Tinted neutrals. Paper, rules and ink lean toward the brand hue instead of pure grey, so text and background read as one material.
    • Dark mode keeps the roles, not the colours. The brand greens become a blue-grey family and the sand accent becomes yellow, each in the same role, with the same geometry between them.
    • Quiet texture. The masthead's faint concentric contour lines evoke topographic and network maps without competing with the title.

    Every number on this page is computed live in your browser from the site's own colour tokens (tokens.css and dark.css): HSL hue and lightness, perceptual OKLCH lightness, and WCAG contrast ratios.

    6Depth

    Depth has two meanings here: how far a surface sits above the page, and how many steps a detail sits below the top of the site.

    Visual depth: layers and shadows

    Surfaces stack in a fixed order, and each layer up casts a darker shadow; the two you handle, flashcards and dialogs, also cast the longest. Shadows are tinted with the darkest brand colour (--shade-rgb: green in light mode, navy in dark mode) instead of black, so they read as shade on the same material rather than a grey stain.

    Structural depth: tab, section, detail

    Every piece of content sits at one of five depths: 0 the tab bar, 1 a tab's numbered sections, 2 a panel, card or deck inside a section, 3 a detail opened from it (a flipped card, a popup, an expanded reference, or a page in a new tab), and 4 a detail of a detail, such as a flashcard's stated-equation popup. The table in section 7 counts the clicks to each kind of detail.

    7Information entropy

    Every click is a choice, and a choice costs attention in proportion to how much it narrows things down. Information theory gives that cost a unit, the bit, so the site's structure can be measured rather than guessed.

    H = −∑i=1n pi log2 pi  bits

    where H is the Shannon entropy of one choice (the information a visitor resolves by making it), n is the number of options on screen, and pi is the probability the visitor wants option i. With no traffic data each option is taken as equally likely (pi = 1/n), which gives the maximum: H = log2 n.

    T = a + b · log2(n + 1)

    The Hick–Hyman law: T is the time to decide among n equally likely options, a is the fixed time to see and respond, and b is the time per bit; both constants are fitted per person and task. Decision time grows with the bits, not the option count, so doubling the options adds one bit, not double the time.

    How much can someone absorb?

    Working memory holds about four chunks at once (Cowan, 2001), a revision of Miller's (1956) "seven, plus or minus two", which Cowan treats as a rough estimate rather than a limit. Options that stay visible, like the tab bar, are recognised rather than remembered, so the limit applies to what a visitor must hold in mind: the ideas in one section. Reading time uses the average adult silent reading rate for non-fiction, 238 words per minute (Brysbaert, 2019).

    How many clicks should a detail take?

    d = ⌈logb N⌉  clicks,  each resolving log2 b bits

    where N is the number of items a visitor might want, b is the breadth (options per click), d is the depth (clicks to reach any one item), and ⌈ ⌉ rounds up. Finding one item among N always takes log2 N bits in total; breadth only decides how those bits are split into clicks. Menu studies found depth costs more than breadth: two levels of about eight beat deeper trees (Miller, 1981; Kiger, 1984), and a three-level web hierarchy was slower than two-level ones (Larson & Czerwinski, 1998). The count itself matters less than confidence: in Porter's (2003) study of 44 users and 620 tasks, the number of clicks did not predict quitting or satisfaction; whether people found what they wanted did.
